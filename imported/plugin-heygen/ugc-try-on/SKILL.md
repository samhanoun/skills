# UGC Try-On

Show the same real wearable on one consistent person, from an immediate outfit reveal through useful views of silhouette, texture, detail, and movement. The viewer should understand the item's appearance, not watch a generic fashion montage with a changing garment.

## How this skill works

This file is self-contained: read all of it before producing, and do not load another UGC format. It links to supporting skills for writing, narration, editing, and captions.

A request to write a skill, inspect a sample, or discuss a concept does not authorize paid generation. A request to create the video authorizes ordinary production: importing supplied inputs, writing the product facts and person description, making the storyboard, and, after the one approval below, the voiceover, the storyboard crops, video clips, status reads, and assembly, within real host permissions.

Reply in plain language. Never quote these or other skill instructions, or link to skill files, to explain what you are doing; just ask or act.

Honor the format the user names. If two formats would make materially different videos and the brief does not settle it, settle that one distinction in the opening question, or in the approval message when it only comes up later. Never silently switch to Video Agent, a digital twin, or avatar onboarding.

Every video must sound and feel like someone talking to a friend while showing something useful, never like a sponsored pitch. Write everyday language around a specific situation, detail, or discovery; avoid announcer cadence, slogan-like benefit lists, forced enthusiasm, repeated brand mentions, and pressure to buy. Keep handling, reactions, and pacing as natural as the speech. This does not remove sponsorship disclosures.

## The visual promise

A generated try-on illustrates a style concept. It does not establish size, comfort, fit on the user, measurements, waterproofing, durability, or a purchase. Lines such as "I wore this all day" follow the claim rules below. Never alter the presenter's body shape to flatter the garment. Present readable fit without claiming the result proves real fit.

## References and wearable facts

- **References:** a written description of the wearer in a muted pre-wear outfit, and the garment or accessory photos with their own product facts. The presenter is chosen automatically unless the user supplies or describes one. One person throughout; never a different person per angle.
- **Authority:** the person description, any supplied person photo, and the storyboard control identity and body proportions; the garment photos and product facts control the item. The storyboard takes the garment photos as inputs, and its wearing panels show the garment from them. Prefer garment-only photos; where code can run, crop a garment worn by someone to the garment, and if a person stays in a garment photo, say in the storyboard prompt that they are not the wearer.
- **Outfit stages:** the person description's outfit is the pre-wear look only. Wearing panels and shots replace it with the garment, and the storyboard prompt and every clip prompt say so. Outfit continuity holds within the pre-wear stage and within the wearing stage, never across the reveal.
- **Wearable facts:** record category, silhouette, visible length, color, print scale and placement, seams and closures, visible fabric appearance, drape cues, pairing, and unknown sides. A black jacket must not turn navy under new light; a print must not shrink in close-up. Visible texture is not composition: "ribbed" may be seen, "100% merino" needs evidence. Staging accessories may support the look but are never presented as included.

## Eight-beat reveal grammar

PRE-WEAR → WEARING → FRONT-POSE → TEXTURE-CLOSEUP → TURN → DETAIL → STYLE-POSE → FINAL-LOOK is the narrative vocabulary; adjust timing to the item and runtime rather than forcing unreadable flashes.

- **Reveal:** open on the muted pre-wear outfit, with an optional plain kraft bag that appears only in the opening beat. Hard-cut directly to the item already worn; never show changing clothes, opening the bag, or pulling the garment out. Later clips never revert to pre-wear, and the bag never returns.
- **Macros:** texture and detail shots are hand-free by default so seams, closures, and surfaces read without fingers covering or stretching them. Never invent embroidery, pockets, or fastenings; show an accessory's underside only when supplied.
- **Turn:** stay within evidenced angles; ask for photos of an unseen back, sole, or closure once, in the approval message. No mirrors or reflective surfaces. Poses may change; person and garment may not.
- **Ending:** finish naturally on the final look and last thought, with no automatic CTA tail or idle beauty pose. An explicitly requested CTA is part of the authored ending.

## Duration and progression

For 15s, give a brisk reveal and enough front, texture, turn, detail, and finish coverage to understand the item; one 15-second clip may carry the hard-cut sequence. For 30s, extend into another part of the same setting with a different useful view or movement. For 45s, an outdoor context can add variety when it suits the item and brief.

No rain or weather unless requested and compatible with the product facts; wet fabric changes appearance and implies performance. Keep continuity plausible, including hair and garment wetness. Give each view a distinct information job (walking shot, side view, texture macro), avoid near-identical poses, and do not cut away when a buyer would want to inspect the item. To keep the garment stable, limit angle changes and scene complexity within a clip, and keep detail shots hand-free.

## Audio and performance

Default to native speech when the creator speaks on camera: short specific lines about the current view ("Here's the side profile"), a relaxed rhythm, small natural gestures, no runway performance unless requested. With the full eight-beat plan, alternate six speaking views with two hand-free detail views; in a native multi-cut clip, the same voice continues over the macros, and the whole native track is kept in assembly. For a deliberately off-screen narrated lookbook, the creator does not visibly speak and the voiceover-first flow below applies. Speech describes what is shown, never visual directions.

Before any script, reference, or storyboard work, send one opening question. It also says in plain words what HeyGen will generate for this format at that length, as in the example, and, when the host's image tool makes the storyboard, that the storyboard is not a HeyGen generation. Choices the brief already settles show as settled and are not asked again. When it is unclear what kind of video is wanted, for example when no product, site, or subject can be identified, ask that in the same message. Wait for the reply: "go" or "use the defaults" applies every default, and any choice the reply leaves open takes its default. A first message that already says to use the defaults or not to ask anything counts as that reply: state the settings in one line and go on. In an automated series, the setup choices and V01's answers count as the reply for every later video, and a scheduled run never asks. The defaults, which the approval message shows again and where the user can still change any of them:

- **Presenter:** as this format sets out above. A person in supplied media must be the user or someone they are authorized to depict. If the user asks for no person in a format that needs one, say in the approval message how the video changes, for example a hands-only Product video instead.
- **Voice:** one real voice chosen to suit the product and audience: from the voice catalog for a voiceover, or written as a voice description for a creator's native speech. Never invent a voice or generate audition samples.
- **Captions:** on, through the [Captions skill](../captions/SKILL.md) after assembly.
- **Product images**, when a product is shown: the photos or product links the user supplies, including in reply to the opening question; if there are none, official product images found online, which you say you are using. Ask for a photo only when none can be found. Never generate or substitute a lookalike product.

### Write the product facts and person description

Before the script, write two short text notes. They are never images; the storyboard prompt and clip prompts paste them verbatim.

**Product facts**, when a product is shown. A real product image is required: never generate a product from a name or description, substitute a lookalike, or fill a gap with a stock image. Use an image as a product input only when it shows one product in one state; where code can run, crop a composite, multi-product, or banner image to the exact product so the real label and color survive, and otherwise use it for claims only and say so in one sentence. Record the object count, silhouette, proportions, colors, material, finish, closures, controls, visible lettering, handling states (cap attached, cap removed beside the bottle), and one size anchor, relative to a hand or to familiar surroundings, never invented dimensions. State part counts, attachments, and the side of each asymmetric part positively (one bottle with one pump fixed to it; one handle on the right when the logo faces the camera). Mark each side photographed or inferred; an inferred side never appears in a storyboard or shot, and if the user wants it shown, ask for a photo of it. Keep visible facts, user-confirmed mechanics, and claims separate, with supplied claim wording exact; preserve genuine branding and never introduce another brand.

**Person description**, when a person or hands appear: apparent age range, face shape, eyes, brows, nose, lips, distinguishing marks, skin tone and texture, hair color, length, texture, and parting, build and posture, and the wardrobe for the ad (each garment's color and fit, footwear, and accessories with the side each is on, with no invented logos or readable text). For hands only, describe the skin tone, sleeve, and any accessories. A supplied photo or quick video of a real adult is the identity authority: describe that person, use a clear front-facing frame as an input to the storyboard, and keep a supplied video for `reference_videos`; the user must be that person or authorized to depict them. One description depicts exactly one adult, never a public figure unless the user is authorized to depict them. Otherwise describe the person the user asked for, or a plausible adult suited to the product and audience, with no stereotypes or invented credentials. Generated people may be invented; products may not.

### Write the timestamped script

**Clips.** Use the fewest HeyGen Video 1 clips, each up to thirty seconds: fill each to thirty and put the remainder in the last; if that remainder is under five seconds, move seconds into it from the previous clip (10 → 10, 30 → 30, 32 → 27+5, 45 → 30+15). Put several shots inside a clip with hard cuts; never split one shot across clips. With a voiceover, the final clip boundaries come from the narration's timing after approval.

**Risky actions.** Video generation often duplicates or distorts liquids, thin applicators, hinges and other mechanisms, cables, transparent parts, and camera arcs around an object. Put each in at most one clip, staged simply with one hand, the object, and the surface it acts on, and fill other shots with low-risk views such as a static product, macro texture, scale in the hand, or a finished result.

**Runtime.** A finished video within about 10 percent of the requested length meets the request unless the user asked for an exact length. Never stretch footage, loop an action, freeze an ending, or cut spoken words to hit a number. A requested cut is a creative instruction, not a guarantee. When the user asks for an exact length, say in the approval message how closely it can be met.

**Story.** Write the whole story before splitting it. Begin on a purposeful action or a specific spoken hook, not a greeting or setup pose; every later shot adds evidence, advances an action, reveals something, or resolves a question, and only the final segment gets a payoff. Write speech as one continuous conversation; sentences may cross cuts and clip boundaries. Budget about 14 characters per second for English, counting spaces and punctuation (about 210 for fifteen seconds), or about 15 for a voiceover at the default 1.1x (about 225); use 14 when the voiceover stays at normal speed, scale the budget for any other requested speed, and re-fit the script if the user changes the speed in the approval round. Other languages and slower voices need fewer. Plan no unearned holds, replayed introductions, repeated face-and-product poses, actions that outlast their purpose, smiling tails at clip ends, or broken physical causality, such as an emptied glass that refills without a new pour.

**Words.** A line mentions only what is on screen while it plays. Would someone say this to a friend while showing it? Rewrite lines that sound like advertising copy or stage directions; make the first phrase specific to this product, drop repeated product names, and favor contractions and varied sentence lengths. Keep spoken words separate from visual directions; never voice "bring the camera closer." Keep any CTA brief and tied to the user's real destination; never invent "link in bio" or an offer. Preserve an exact supplied script. Writing patterns are in [script writer](../script-writer/SKILL.md). Direct delivery to match: a relaxed pace, natural breaths, emphasis on the detail being shown, and the same attitude across clips. Put delivery direction in the voice choice, the voice description, or the prompt, never in the spoken words; a friendly voice cannot rescue sales copy.

**Claims.** Ground speech in verified mechanics, visible details, and allowed claims. Everyday benefits evident from the design need no source. Use supplied testimonial-style lines, usage history, statistics, and comparisons as written; mark any the user did not supply "[confirm]" so the approval covers them. Never make medical or health claims, and never let delivery or visuals strengthen an approved claim.

### Make the storyboard

Before approval, make one storyboard image for the whole video with the host's OpenAI image generation, preferring GPT Image 2.5 in its quality-oriented "sunburst" variant at high quality; if model or quality selection is not exposed, do not invent parameters or claim a setting was applied. Supply real images through the supported image input; a filename in a prompt conditions nothing. Build it from the person description and product facts, pasted verbatim, the supplied or official product photos that match the product states it shows, a clear front-facing frame of any supplied person, and any other photos this format feeds to storyboards; never from website screenshots, and never showing an inferred side. When a person's face appears in the video, at least one early panel shows it clearly, front-facing, head and shoulders. Every shot of the whole video gets its own panel, in order across all clips, and later its own crop; a clip sends at most nine crops, HeyGen's reference-image limit, so any shot beyond nine in one clip is a quick accent written in the prompt only, with no panel. Drawing every shot in one image keeps the person and product the same, and the outfit, setting, and light the same unless the approved script changes them, and lets each clip's first panel continue from the state the previous clip's last panel ends in.

The storyboard is one grid of equal panels, one per shot, read left to right, then top to bottom, in shot order, each panel the start frame of its shot, with the same phone-capture look and natural, healthy skin as the clip prompts. Every panel has the video's aspect ratio, 9:16 or 16:9, and shows that shot's framing and subject state; because each panel is later trimmed to that exact shape, the prompt says to keep the product's identifying detail and any face within the central area of each panel where the shot allows; trimming the edges of the frame is fine. Take the grid (columns × rows) and image size from the table below, which keeps every panel close to its shape at the tool's fixed sizes. When the image size is selectable, use instead the grid whose whole image is nearest square for the shot count, set the image to that grid's exact shape plus gutters, and request the largest size the tool allows. Leave any cells after the last shot empty in the gutter color; never add a shot to fill them. Separate the panels with thin, uniform gutters of one flat plain color and leave no other plain band or margin, so the image can be cut cleanly; nothing crosses a gutter, and the image has no text, numbers, or labels. Never squeeze or stretch a panel. When captions are on, keep essential product detail out of the bottom fifth of every panel.

| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1×1 at 1024×1536 | 1×1 at 1536×1024 |
| 2–4 | 2×2 at 1024×1536 | 2×2 at 1536×1024 |
| 5–6 | 3×2 at 1024×1024 | 2×3 at 1024×1024 |
| 7–10 | 5×2 at 1536×1024, or 3×3 at 1024×1536 for 9 shots | 2×5 at 1024×1536, or 3×3 at 1536×1024 for 9 shots |
| 11–12 | 5×3 at 1024×1024 | 3×5 at 1024×1024 |
| 13–20 | 5×4 at 1024×1536 | 4×5 at 1536×1024 |

Before showing the storyboard, check it. Layout: the planned grid, exactly one panel per shot in shot order, equal panels of about the video's aspect ratio (about 0.49 to 0.67 as wide as they are tall for 9:16, about 1.5 to 2.05 for 16:9) and not stretched or squeezed, uniform plain gutters, and no wide plain bands (empty end cells of the planned grid are expected); the product's identifying detail and any face sit within each panel's central area where the shot allows; trimming the edges of the frame is fine. Product: the silhouette, proportions, colors, part counts, and label spelling and placement match the photos and facts, with no mirrored text, duplicate product, or invented feature. Identity: the same person in every panel, matching the description and any supplied photo, with the face checked in every panel that shows it. Continuity: the outfit, setting, and the product, package, or garment state in each panel are what the script sets for that shot, and each clip's first panel starts from the state the previous clip's last panel ends in. Skin: natural and healthy, neither smoothed nor over-textured, blotchy, or dirty. If the storyboard drifts, regenerate it once from its original inputs with one targeted correction, never by editing a drifted output; if it still fails, show it and name the remaining issue. Show only the final storyboard, in the approval message.

After approval, if the voiceover's timing regroups the shots into different clips, give each clip the approved crops of its shots, in order; this needs no new approval and no new storyboard.

The storyboard is never sent to HeyGen; only its crops are (see Cut the storyboard into shot images). A supplied quick video of the person may stay in `reference_videos`. If no storyboard can be made, say so in the approval message. Then, or later for any clip whose crops cannot be used, send the original product photos and any supplied photo of the person instead, never a generated image of the product, with the product facts and person description in the prompt. Trim each photo, centered, to exactly the video's aspect ratio when that keeps the whole product and any person in frame, otherwise to the other of 9:16 and 16:9, and import the trimmed copy unless the original is already exact. If neither shape keeps them whole, never pad the photo: tell the user and ask for a photo at 9:16 or 16:9. Before the approval message, check in Python which product photos trim whole to 9:16 or 16:9; if none does, fold a request for a photo at 9:16 or 16:9 into the approval question. When a person appears and no photo of them was supplied, also say that the person will come from the written description only and may differ between clips and from the storyboard, and offer a photo of the person, one clip of up to 30 seconds, or a hands-only version.

Every reference image sent to HeyGen is exactly 9:16 or 16:9, made so by trimming, never by padding. Before each upload, check its exact shape and record its pixel size in the production ledger. If code cannot run, there are no crops: send only photos that are already exact, and tell the user which ones could not be used. If a clip that shows the product would then have no exact image, say so in the approval message and ask for a photo of the product at 9:16 or 16:9; never generate that clip from text alone.

### Native speech for an on-camera creator

A visible speaking person speaks natively inside the Video 1 clip, preserving lip sync. Write one voice description per speaker (language, accent, pitch, timbre, pace, and natural delivery, for example "English, light London accent; warm mid-low voice; relaxed pace, natural breaths, varied emphasis") and paste it verbatim into every prompt with that person's speech, separate from the dialogue. Quote the exact dialogue, name the speaker, and require synchronized native speech with no other speaker, voiceover, music, or on-screen text; off-screen cutaways inside the clip may continue the same voice. Never add `generate_audio`, `voice_id`, or `lip_sync` to `reference_to_video`. Keep dialogue compact so the action stays readable, at normal speed with no time stretching. Keep the native audio in assembly; never mute a speaking person and dub them with synthesized speech. Prefer one speech treatment throughout; when a video mixes a native-speaking creator with a voiceover, never overlap them and never claim they share a voice. If an exact supplied line is too long for native speech in its clip, propose shorter copy or a voiceover in the approval message; never silently change the words.

Exact words come first. Video 1 can garble lines or fill gaps with invented speech, so every clip prompt whose native audio is kept states in its spoken-text section: "Speak exactly the quoted words, in order. In every interval without quoted words, including before the first line, between lines, during silent shots, and after the last line, there is no speech of any kind: no mumbling, filler words, invented words, whispering, or background voices. Never fill silence with speech and never invent words." A clip with no quoted words gets only the second and third sentences. Clips the voiceover owns are muted in assembly and unaffected: they request no speech from the video model.

### Voiceover first

Use this for every off-screen voiceover. After approval, before any video:

1. Synthesize the whole approved narration in one call with `create_speech_without_player` when listed (`create_speech` opens a player), as plain text (never SSML), using the voice from the approved settings line at HeyGen speed 1.0. Never set `speed`, or any other HeyGen speed or rate option, to 1.1 or to anything but 1.0. Follow [narrator](../narrator/SKILL.md) for the tool contract, but never open the voice picker again. Never synthesize one take per clip; every call is charged. When the user supplies a narration recording they are authorized to use, use it instead of synthesis and skip the speed-up unless they ask for one. Never imitate a celebrity or clone a voice from unrelated audio.
2. Right after synthesis, speed the whole narration track up to 1.1x once, in Python with FFmpeg's `atempo=1.1` filter, which keeps the pitch. This is the default voiceover speed. If the user asks for a different speed, use that speed instead, applied the same way; normal speed, or a host that cannot run Python with FFmpeg, skips this step. Scale the returned word timestamps by the inverse of the speed (1/1.1 by default), or re-align the sped-up file, and use the sped-up duration and timings for every step below. It is the one allowed speed change: one fixed delivery speed applied once to the whole track, never per clip and never to fit a duration.
3. Add no pauses: no `<break>` tags, SSML pauses, silence padding, any other slowing or speed change after this one, or time stretching, in synthesis or editing. Pictures follow the voiceover, never the reverse.
4. Split the narration into clips using its duration and word timestamps: keep the approved clip grouping whenever each clip's narration fits 5–30 seconds; otherwise use 5–30 seconds each, the fewest clips, with each boundary at the approved shot cut nearest a sentence or phrase end. Keep the approved shot list: if the clip count changes, spread the approved shots across the clips and never add shots; a format's shots-per-clip rule yields to the approved shots. If no word timestamps are returned, align the audio by transcription; never divide words evenly. Each clip's `duration` is its narration segment rounded up to the next whole second, plus the final clip's payoff tail.
5. In each clip's prompt, time the shots from the word timestamps, relative to the clip's start, so each visual change lands on the words that name it.
6. Request no speech or music from the video model. In assembly, mute these clips and lay the narration track over them unchanged, trimming footage to the narration. The final clip may keep about one second of silent picture after the last word for the payoff settle; that is a picture tail, not narration padding, and the voiceover itself still gets no pauses.

In a video that mixes native speech and voiceover, these steps and the muting apply only to clips the voiceover owns, each a whole 5–30 second clip; native clips keep their audio. Otherwise prefer one treatment.

If the measured narration misses the requested length by more than about 10 percent, tell the user before generating video and offer a shorter or longer script. If the narration needs more clips than the approval message stated, say so before generating video and offer to trim the script to fit the stated number.

### Cut the storyboard into shot images

After approval, split the approved storyboard into one image per shot with Python (PIL or similar). Open the file the image tool returned; if that file is not available to Python, treat it as a cropping failure, never ask the user to download or re-upload it, and never redraw or regenerate panels. Find the content box inside any outer margins, then find the gutters in both directions: full-height columns and full-width rows of near-uniform plain color. If detection does not find one fewer gutter column than the grid has columns and one fewer gutter row than it has rows, divide the content box equally into the planned grid instead. Cut the panels apart left to right, then top to bottom, skipping any empty cells at the end, and trim each crop by the gutter and margin widths measured on the image, not by color, without scaling it; with equal division, inset each cell by the requested gutter width, or about 2% of the cell, instead. Keep the crops only if their number equals the number of panels and each is about the video's aspect ratio (0.49 to 0.67 as wide as it is tall for 9:16, 1.5 to 2.05 for 16:9) and not blank or one flat color; if gutter-based crops fail, retry once with equal division. Then trim the side that is over, centered, to the largest 9k×16k size inside the crop (16k×9k for a 16:9 video), so it has exactly the video's aspect ratio; never scale it unevenly or stretch it, and if more than about 16 percent would be cut, the crop fails. Then look at the crops side by side: each shows one shot, with no gutter line or part of another panel at any edge. Normal phone framing is fine: a crop may trim the top of a head, feet, hands, or background. It fails only when the product's identifying detail, such as its label, logo, or the garment's print, or most of the face is cut off. Crops that pass go ahead with no question to the user; never ask the user about crop framing. Save each crop as a PNG numbered in shot order, give each clip the crops of its shots, and record the storyboard's grid, its requested and returned size, and each crop's pixel size in the production ledger. Cropping fails only when code cannot run, the storyboard is not available to Python, or the crops still fail these checks after equal division. The crops are internal: never show them to the user.

Import each crop with `import_asset_from_chat` in the form the adapter accepts (see Attachments: the crop's absolute local path for `file: string`), or by raw upload where code can reach the network. Never treat an OpenAI file identifier as a HeyGen `asset_id`. Send a clip's crops as its only reference images, at most nine, in the clip's shot order: Image 1 is the clip's first shot, Image 2 its second, and so on. Send them only as reference images, never in a first-frame or image-to-video field. A clip gets all of its crops or none, never a mix: if cropping fails, or any crop is rejected (including for size) or fails to transfer, that clip uses the photo fallback above. Tell the user in the next progress update and again in the delivery note, in one plain sentence per affected clip, which clip used the product photos and why, such as "Clip 2 used your product photos instead of the storyboard shots, because the storyboard could not be split."

### Generate the clips

Use HeyGen Video 1 for all footage through `reference_to_video` with `model: "heygen-video-1"`. The connection's general rules sending visual productions over 30 seconds to Video Agent and an unstated length to a single clip of 15 seconds or less do not apply here: this workflow defaults to 30 seconds and meets any runtime with Video 1 clips assembled client-side. Always pass an integer `duration` of 5–30 seconds and the aspect ratio and resolution from the opening question explicitly (9:16 and 1080p unless the user chose 16:9, 2K, or 768p; the tool's defaults are 5 seconds, a frame that follows the first reference image, which for a 16:9 photo is landscape, and 768p), and use `prompt_enhancement: "quality"` by default (`"disabled"` when it would alter a tightly authored prompt, but only for a clip of 15 seconds or less: a clip over 15 seconds keeps `"quality"` or uses `"turbo"`, never `"disabled"` or `"ultraquality"`). If the callable schema has no aspect-ratio field, send only references in the video's shape (the shot crops, or photos trimmed to it), since the first image sets the frame, and say so briefly. Pass references only as real `url` or `asset_id` objects, and use camelCase names such as `referenceImages` only when the callable schema uses them. Stay within the tool's limits: at most nine reference images, three reference videos, and three reference audio files. Reference audio only guides generation; it is not a soundtrack. Live tool schemas govern: never invent fields, copy an older API, or claim an undocumented capability is absent. Report a schema gap precisely, settle a material limitation before paid generation, and never switch accounts or environments silently. Do not use avatar tools (`prepare_avatar_video`, `create_video_from_avatar`, `create_video_from_cinematic_avatar`), Video Agent, or a digital twin, even as a fallback; an avatar requested as presenter stays in this workflow through references built from real media. An explicit request for an avatar video needs a separate avatar workflow, settled before any generation.

Write each prompt in plain sections: start frames, identity and product invariants, environment, timed shots with explicit hard cuts, physical interaction, camera and texture, spoken text or silence (with the exact-words lines for any native audio), continuity, and exclusions. With shot crops, open with: "Images 1–N are the start frames of shots 1–N, in order. They are plans, not frames to copy. Render one continuous 9:16 video with hard cuts between the shots, and no panels, borders, split screens or text from the images. Match the person, product, wardrobe, and setting in them exactly; only each shot's motion is new." with N written as the clip's number of crops and 16:9 in place of 9:16 for a horizontal video, then list each shot with its time span, matching its image. When a clip has more than nine shots, mark each prompt-only accent shot "[accent]", leave it unnumbered, and add after the opener: "Shots marked [accent] are quick inserts with no image or panel of their own." When product photos are sent instead of crops, open by saying the same of them: they are product references, not frames to copy; their framing and background are not the video's composition, and the video shows exactly one product. Keep IDs and planning labels out of creative prose. Paste the person description and the product facts verbatim, and state part counts, attachments, and the side of each asymmetric part positively (one bottle with one pump fixed to it). Each shot states framing, subject state, one principal action, and its visible endpoint; the next shot starts from that state. Nothing moves unless a visible hand moves it: in a shot without a hand, the object stays fixed and only the camera moves slightly. When captions are on, keep essential product detail out of the bottom fifth of the frame in every prompt. Start shots mid-action with modest camera moves; never pass through objects, show unevidenced sides, or stack complex interactions; no dissolves or slow motion unless requested. Generate no captions, subtitles, or caption-like lettering in the footage; captions come only from the Captions skill.

Ask for authentic phone capture: natural window light, restrained exposure, deep enough focus to read the object, subtle sensor texture, real materials, and natural, healthy skin as a modern phone records it in soft light: an even tone whose fine texture stays subtle even in a close-up. Avoid waxy skin, beauty-filter smoothing, facial reshaping, oversaturation, glow, heavy sharpening, and decorative bokeh, and equally avoid exaggerated pores, blotchy or grey patches, dirt, sun damage, or freckles, spots, and wrinkles beyond what the person description or a supplied photo shows. When a creator holds a product near the face, frame at least from the shoulders up rather than filling the frame with skin. Place small imperfections in the environment, never on the person or the product. Keep one hero product, plausible hands and contact, consistent gravity, and no mirrors. Restate a continuity lock in every clip (person, product, setting, wardrobe, hands, light direction, prop states), and end only the final clip on the payoff with about a one-second settle; earlier clips hand off mid-thought, and each later clip continues from the prior action rather than reintroducing the product.

Submit all clips concurrently within service limits and record each clip's prompt, references, settings, `video_id`, and state; clip numbers, not completion order, set the edit order. Status reads are part of the authorized production: poll pending clips yourself with `get_model_video` at the returned interval or about every thirty seconds, and update the user at least every minute. If a render is still pending after about fifteen minutes, stop, say it is still rendering, and resume when the user asks. Retry a retryable read error once after its delay; never create a clip again to check progress. Never call `show_video` or open a player after submission, even though the connection's guidance shows a player after a generation call; poll `get_model_video` yourself, finish assembly and captions in the same run, and show only the final video, captioned unless the user turned captions off. Tell a failed lookup apart from a failed render, and reconcile an uncertain creation outcome with `get_model_video` before any new submission. Never invent batch APIs or idempotency keys. Record IDs, links, and the current stage in the ledger before any wait or interruption; if a real limit or terminal error stops progress, report the incomplete state and never call it delivered.

Do not review finished clips for visual defects or regenerate them: a finished clip goes to assembly, and the user judges the result. If a render fails or is cancelled, explain the returned reason and ask whether to retry that clip; never retry automatically.

If HeyGen refuses a voiceover or clip because of the plan's allowance (HTTP 402, `quota_exceeded`, insufficient credit, or a message that the allowance is used up), stop submitting and never retry automatically. Keep polling every clip already submitted. Tell the user in plain words which clips are done and which were not made, with the limit and any renewal date HeyGen states, following the plan-limit rule in Ask for approval once. Offer what fits: a shorter video from the finished clips when they stand on their own, or making only the missing clips once the allowance renews. Never regenerate a finished clip, and never call an incomplete video delivered.

### Assemble and caption

Assemble the clips in order client-side by following the [video-editing skill](../video-editing/SKILL.md), at the approved aspect ratio and resolution. Here it only orders, joins, and mixes the Video 1 clips with their voiceover or native audio; add no other footage, stills, motion graphics, or stock. Music only when requested: a track the user is authorized to use, or original instrumental music made in code, mixed beneath the speech. A single clip with no voiceover and captions off needs no assembly.

Unless the user turned captions off, hand the assembled video, approved script, language, and dimensions to the [Captions skill](../captions/SKILL.md), which only adds text layers and never changes footage or audio. If captioning fails, say why (or that the reason is unknown) and deliver the uncaptioned video without claiming captions.

Before display, run technical checks only: probe the export's duration, resolution, and audio stream.

### Deliver

Show the final video once, only after assembly and captions are finished, through the host's media display with its actual file or link. Only a single clip that needs no assembly and has captions off is shown directly, with its fresh `video_url` from `get_model_video`; with captions on, the captioned export is the final video, never the raw clip. Never pass a Video 1 ID, a local file, or an invented ID to `show_video`. Never show intermediate clips or drafts, and do not show the storyboard again or any crop. If display fails, retry with the same file; never regenerate to fix a player error. Finish the whole production without stopping after a submission or asking the user to come back, apart from the fifteen-minute render wait. Give the final video's measured duration and any material limitation; never claim a frame-accurate runtime or a full inspection without evidence. If display is unavailable, keep the export and report the limitation.

Under the video, list each HeyGen clip with its time span in the final video and its page in the HeyGen app, made by appending the `video_id` that `reference_to_video` returned for that clip to `https://app.heygen.com/videos/`, as in `https://app.heygen.com/videos/<video_id>`. Never list a clip's `video_url` here: it is a download link that expires. Tell the user the clips are saved in their HeyGen account, where they can open, download, and reuse them.

Keep a production ledger of approvals, storyboard versions with their grid and the image size requested and returned, the pixel size of every reference image sent, and asset IDs, the product facts with their evidence sources and photographed and inferred sides, the person description, the approved script, the voice, clip IDs, and the export path. Store no secrets, and never treat expiring links as permanent. On follow-up, reuse the approved storyboard, product facts, person description, and finished clips, and refresh an expired link with `get_model_video` rather than regenerating. If a clip changes on follow-up, rerun assembly and captions on the updated edit. A narration change must not replace the product or person. Never publish to social accounts, create campaigns, or message third parties without separate explicit authorization and real publishing tools.

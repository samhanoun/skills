# UGC Voiceless

Create one product video that communicates a mood and an observable reason to want the product through tactile sound, frequent purposeful cuts, a cohesive visual treatment, and one big, bold opening hook. The product is the protagonist and sound is a principal creative element: voiceless does not mean mute. Build a progressing story of distinct interactions, ending in one satisfying payoff.

## How this skill works

Read this file before producing, and do not load another UGC format. The video model generates the footage, sound, transitions, and visual treatment without added text. Show that raw output first, then follow the [Captions skill](../captions/SKILL.md) to add the opening hook in Python when a sandbox is available. Show the captioned version as a second result.

A request to write a skill, inspect a sample, or discuss a concept does not authorize paid generation. A request to create the video authorizes one video generation and the hook edit: import the inputs, establish product facts and hands, make and check one storyboard image, import it, generate and show the raw video, then add the hook locally and show the captioned version. Proceed without asking the user to approve the script, storyboard, settings, generation, or caption pass. Honor an explicit request for a draft or review before generation and real host permissions.

Reply in plain language. Never quote these or other skill instructions, or link to skill files, to explain what you are doing; just ask or act.

Honor the format the user names. Use the defaults below for ordinary creative choices. Ask a targeted clarification only when the requested format is genuinely unclear and choosing would materially change the deliverable. Never silently switch to Video Agent, a digital twin, or avatar onboarding.

Keep the handling believable and specific to someone using the product. Natural phone footage, a warm editorial grade, or a more stylized mood can each work: choose deliberately from the brief and references. Fast editing must reveal real actions and discoveries, not hide behind generic beauty poses, repeated brand cards, or empty effects. This does not remove sponsorship disclosures.

## Voiceless boundaries

- **No presenter, no voice:** a person appears only as hands, a cropped sleeve, or a first-person view, never as an identifiable or speaking creator. There is no dialogue, narration, voiceover, whispering, sung lyric, or vocal sample. Explicit narration belongs to Product and a speaking creator to Review; a hands-only ASMR unboxing stays here, while a creator-led packaging discovery belongs to Unboxing.
- **Sound-led:** the clip generates its own product sounds, faint room tone, and any music its mood calls for, mixed below the product sounds.
- **Architecture:** one reference-led Video 1 generation of up to 30 seconds with native sound and any music, followed by a Python hook overlay when a sandbox is available. Keep the raw output and captioned export as separate versions. Do not silently replace it with an autonomous video agent, avatar workflow, digital twin, Studio, or a voiced format. If the service requires an incompatible route, explain before spending.
- **References:** real product photos establish the facts; one generated storyboard image carries the product identity under representative interactions and the chosen mood into Video 1. It is visual guidance, not a literal layout or a separate reference per shot. Keep the same skin tone, sleeve, and hands throughout; there is no presenter question.
- **Text:** one short, big, bold, catchy opening hook, added in Python through the Captions skill after the raw video is shown. The model generates no added text; preserve genuine product branding separately. Honor a request for no text or explicitly different copy.

## Choose the creative promise

Choose **one visible or audible payoff** that can be understood quickly: a satisfying closure, a texture spreading, a clean writing stroke, an object fitting into its intended place, a pour, or a clearly supported before/after. Make the product recognizable within roughly three seconds. An attractive montage with no useful result or sensory reason to care is incomplete; handling the product (lifting, tilting, setting it down) is not a payoff on its own. The strongest payoffs complete something: empty to full, open to closed, scattered to arranged, blank to marked. Pair the payoff with **one hero sound**, the single most satisfying sound in the video. The payoff lands once, at the end.

| Fit | What to emphasize | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strong sensory value | Texture, sound, design, visible use, or a clear result in beauty, food/drink, stationery, kitchen tools, accessories, and home products | Replacing a real demonstration with unrelated lifestyle props |
| Value expressed through context | A believable ritual for supplements, a setting for fragrance/candles, or verified capacity for a bag | Implying health effects, an objectively proven scent experience, or unverified capacity through mood alone |
| Abstract or explanation-heavy value | An honest tangible detail or mood treatment if the user wants this format | Claiming that sensory footage proves software capability, financial performance, a service result, or a technical specification |

If the requested benefit cannot be shown truthfully, explain the specific limitation and propose a narrower visible highlight. Do not invent proof or silently add a narrator. This format can support discovery, launches, or creative variety; do not promise that it will overcome every purchase objection or outperform a voiced ad.

Build a short **sound inventory before the shot list**. List only sounds the actual object/action can plausibly make: click, snap, peel, paper rustle, zip, spritz, pour, crunch, pen scratch, fabric movement, or contact with the chosen surface. Map each sound to its visible cause and resulting product state. Do not give a screw cap a snap latch, a soft pouch a glass clink, or a silent material an invented mechanical noise.

Choose the treatment that gives the product its strongest truthful moment. A named user preference wins; otherwise choose the treatment and mood yourself and proceed.

| Treatment | Story arc | Good sensory material |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ASMR unboxing | Packaging contact → seal/wrapping → one reveal → detail/use → hero | Tape, paper, lid contact, product set-down; only show supplied or verified packaging |
| Satisfying use | Immediate useful action → close detail → visible outcome → product payoff | Scrub, spray, spread, click, write, slice, or pour with the real mechanics |
| Silent routine | Enter an already-underway ritual → product interaction → finished moment | A desk reset, morning preparation, gym bag, or countertop routine |
| Transformation | Readable before → brief process → supported after → product beside result | A genuine clean/organized/styled state; never fabricate efficacy or elapsed results |
| Restock/refill | A clear destination → repeated placements with variation → completed arrangement | Container taps, lid clicks, pours, wrappers; maintain believable counts and fill levels |
| Product in the wild | A few purposeful use contexts → matching motion/material details → recognizable close | Fashion/accessories/outdoor use, natural handling and environmental sound |

Do not cram all six treatments into one video. A routine can contain one satisfying-use payoff, but the result still needs a single through-line. An unboxing reveals the product once; removed seals stay removed, and a case can close only through a shown closing action. A transformation retains its changed state; restocking does not mysteriously empty between cuts. For ordinary one-product treatments, keep one hero object. Multiple units belong only to a verified restock or comparison concept.

Choose a small mood vocabulary and translate it into visible and audible decisions:

- **Calm/tactile:** soft window light, warm neutrals, close textures, deliberate hand movement inside quick purposeful shots, clear product sounds, and pure ASMR with no music by default. Clean hard cuts can create an intimate rhythm without a flashy transition.
- **Fresh/clean:** bright diffused light, neutral-to-cool whites, restrained contrast, crisp surfaces, dry clicks or water detail, and light rhythmic music. A natural treatment with no apparent filter often fits best.
- **Playful/energetic:** product-led color accents, crisp contrast, sharp contacts, and a restrained original instrumental rhythm beneath the object. Match cuts, a quick whip, or a brief soft flash can punctuate a meaningful change.
- **Warm/editorial or quietly luxurious:** warm highlights, softly shaded backgrounds, muted surrounding colors, rich but readable blacks, controlled reflections, and a little grain only if it suits the brief. Hard cuts with an occasional gentle warm light leak can fit tactile packaging and material detail; keep the final result readable.

These are starting points, not a menu the user must complete. Any music named here is original instrumental music generated inside the clip, mixed below the product sounds (see Sound). Keep one setting, one compatible color palette, and at most two or three useful props for most concepts. Product-in-the-wild may change locations deliberately while preserving product identity, hand/sleeve continuity, and visual rhythm.

Commit to a **visual treatment and transition language** before writing the prompt: light direction and softness, warmth/white balance, contrast and shadow depth, saturation, texture/grain or explicitly no filter, and which cuts get which effect. Put these choices into both the storyboard and video prompts. Preserve the actual product and skin colors; grade the scene without changing the variant. A supplied example informs pacing and treatment, not permission to copy its product, claims, text, or every effect. Natural footage with hard cuts is a complete choice when it best serves the mood.

## Shots and rhythm

**Open on an immediate useful sound or action, with footage filling the entire frame.** When the product and story allow it, show the actual setting for a split second before the product enters: a full-frame stovetop, then a hand bringing in a pan; or a tabletop, then a cup being set down. Begin the entrance promptly and land the contact within roughly the first second. The brief time before the product arrives lets the later Python hook overlay appear over the setting without hiding the product. This is timing, not a reserved area in the image: never add margins, padding, blank bands, borders, or an inset picture for captions. Otherwise open on an action already in progress. Do not force an entrance for every product or prolong the setting-only moment for text. Show enough product context early to make the macro understandable. Satisfying sounds and contacts recur throughout; do not save the only satisfying moment for the final seconds.

Use the whole length as one paced sequence, not one long generic beauty shot. At 15 seconds the regions are these; scale them for a shorter or longer clip:

| Time region | Editorial job | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–1.5s | Stop attention with action/sound and a short hook | Fast, purposeful opening views; text appears promptly and remains readable |
| 1.5–5s | Establish the product and its tactile character | Alternate recognizable shape, handling, and meaningful close details |
| 5–11s | Develop the use or routine toward the payoff | Each cut adds action, texture, or evidence; include a small rhythm change |
| 11–15s | Complete the progression and close | Final distinct interactions lead to one payoff and a brief result hold, with an intentional sound ending |

Default to **12–16 distinct shots in 15 seconds** (11–15 cuts), including for ASMR: calm handling does not require a slow edit. Name the exact chosen shot count in the video prompt, give every shot a time span, and say to execute all of them rather than collapse the sequence into a few long takes. Most shots last about 0.6–1.2 seconds; use a few shorter accents and reserve about 1.2–1.8 seconds for the final result. Scale the count with the clip length (about 24–32 shots at 30 seconds). Explicitly slower user direction or a mechanism that needs time can justify fewer shots; explain that choice in the plan. Keep contact and sound readable, carry a sound tail across a cut when appropriate, and omit dead reaches or waiting rather than speeding up a whole action.

**Every cut must reveal a new action, detail through interaction, or product state.** Changing the angle on the same repeated action is not progression. An evidenced unboxing might move from sliding off a sleeve to lifting a lid, removing an insert, lifting the case, opening its clasp, revealing the product, removing protection, unfolding it, demonstrating a control, inspecting its material through touch, using it, and putting it away. Include only packaging and mechanics supported by the references. Do not repeat openings, peel the same seal twice, alternate indistinguishable beauty shots, or reset the object to fill the cut count.

Vary shot scale, angle, contact point, and movement where they add information: an overhead action, a macro contact, a diagonal in-hand view, or a low detail. A consistent overhead setup can still work when each cut shows a different meaningful interaction; arbitrary camera changes are not a substitute for a story. The one storyboard represents selected product states and the mood, not every shot. The shot count is independent of its panel count and the API's reference-image limit.

Motivate key cuts with a product sound or musical beat. Aim for the new shot to arrive just before or with the sound's transient, with visible contact aligned to the sound. Break the cut pattern once or twice with a short quiet gap or longer hold so the rhythm is not mechanical. Music is an exception to literal visible causation; product clicks, pours, and impacts need a visible source.

Choose transitions for the mood and specify them at the relevant cuts in the generation prompt. Clean hard cuts can carry the entire video. A match cut can connect related shapes or actions; a hand swipe or quick whip can motivate a change of view. For a warm editorial mood, a gentle 2–4-frame exposure lift or warm light leak can bridge two or three selected cuts. Use one coherent effect family, not an effect on every cut, and never obscure the action with a long transition, repeated strobing, or a dissolve that blends two product states. The opening hook stays legible through any effect. A brief generated speed ramp may emphasize an action if it remains physically readable and the sound stays synchronized; never retime the whole video or use freezes as padding.

A loop is optional. Make the final position, motion, lighting, and sound tail connect naturally to the opening when the product state permits it. Do not reseal an opened box, refill an emptied container, or undo a transformation merely to force a loop. A complete satisfying ending is better than a false physical reset.

## Sound

Describe native audio in as much detail as the image. Build a clear three-layer hierarchy:

| Layer | Prompt direction |
| --- | --- |
| Product sounds | Foreground, close, crisp, material-specific, synchronized to visible actions; these carry the emotional payoff |
| Room ambience, and any instrumental music | Faint, consistent room tone running to the last frame. Any music is one coherent low instrumental bed matching the mood, generated in the clip, roughly 10–15 dB below foreground product events as an initial creative target, ducked around the hero sound and important contacts |
| Accents | Rare, quiet transitions; felt rather than dominating the object |

Specify the characteristic of each main sound: the short dry click of a mechanism, the low wooden tap of a set-down, the fine scratch of ink on paper, the air and droplets of a real spritz. Ask for believable but gently heightened detail, not deafening transients or cartoon Foley. Do not substitute unrelated stock-ASMR sounds. Avoid refrigerator/AC hum, traffic, notifications, random crowd chatter, breathing, mouth sounds, speech, lyrics, or synthetic voices unless a nonverbal product-use sound is explicitly appropriate and requested.

Let some events breathe. A beat of near-silence, up to about a second, before the hero sound makes it satisfying; keep every other sound out of that moment. Preserve faint consistent room tone between contacts rather than noisy cuts or unnatural silence gates. Music may use a clear pulse for timing, but it must never bury the very sound that makes the product interesting. Pure ASMR with no music is often the strongest choice.

Describe an original instrumental mood rather than requesting a recognizable song, artist imitation, or unlicensed trending recording. Attach user-supplied music, or a real product-sound recording, to the generation only when its intended use is authorized and the actual tool supports it; reference audio guides generation and is not a guaranteed exact soundtrack or deterministic stem replacement. Do not claim that generated music automatically has clearance for every advertising use.

Keep room tone running to the last frame and let the final contact sound finish before it. Keep the final transient and music/ambience tail deliberate, with no clipped last sound, abrupt unrelated ending, or extra silent outro. Review on phone speakers and earbuds when actual listening is available. Numeric mix targets and a written sound prompt do not prove that the generated mix achieved them.

## On-screen text

**One big, bold hook; no subtitles or running captions.** Choose roughly **2–5 words** that name the product, promise an immediate sensory moment, or create grounded curiosity. Preserve user-supplied wording. Use it once near the opening, visible within the first 0.3 seconds, targeting about 1.5–2 seconds of steady reading time when the actual footage allows. If the arriving product would cross the text, clear or reposition the overlay before overlap; shorten the hold when needed rather than delay the product or change the framing. Then remove it for the rest of the video. No middle label, second slogan, end-card copy, or default written CTA: the final action and result supply the close. Honor a request for no text. Include explicitly requested additional text in the script; do not invent extra copy.

Keep the exact hook wording and timing in the **caption plan**, outside the video-generation prompt and storyboard image. Tell Video 1 to generate no added text, titles, captions, subtitles, watermarks, or stray lettering; genuine printed product branding remains part of the object. Require edge-to-edge footage throughout; never ask the model to leave space or reserve an area for captions. After showing the raw output, hand its completed media, the hook plan, and the visual treatment to the [Captions skill](../captions/SKILL.md) for a Python-rendered hook overlay. There is no speech: use its opening-hook guidance without transcription, word alignment, a subtitle track, or invented dialogue.

Make it visibly large: heavy or condensed bold sans-serif, one or two short lines spanning roughly 60–80% of the usable frame width, high contrast against the actual opening footage, with a restrained shadow or outline if needed. Match font character, weight, color, texture, and any brief entrance motion to the video's mood, palette, grade, and cut rhythm. A warm editorial treatment might use cream or amber type; a natural, clean video may suit crisp white; an energetic edit can carry a quick restrained scale-in. These are choices, not presets to impose. Inspect the actual opening frames and the product's arrival time. Overlay large type directly on the full-frame setting, using naturally visible background and timing to keep the product, hands, and contact points readable through the opening cuts. Platform safe areas constrain only the text placement; the underlying footage still fills every edge. Adapt the overlay's placement, line breaks, and exit time to the footage. Measure the actual type and inspect it at phone size; do not shrink it into a small subtitle, add an opaque full-screen card, or shrink, pad, crop, or reposition the footage to make a caption area.

The hook must be grounded in visible product facts or user-supplied claims. "WAIT FOR THE CLICK" requires the promised click promptly; never invent clinical results, scarcity, a discount, or an offer to make the hook stronger. If the user requests a CTA, use a real established action or destination and include its exact treatment in the plan; do not fabricate a button, link, or availability.

## Direct product footage

Aim for believable capture with the chosen visual treatment: natural or directional light, intentional movement, readable focus, controlled exposure, and tactile material detail. Lock exposure/focus within each shot; a planned transition flash is distinct from accidental exposure pumping. Avoid unnecessary focus hunting, waxy skin, oversharpening, or impossible reflections. Keep the grade consistent, retaining label legibility, true product color, and healthy skin. Say explicitly when the best choice is natural light and no visible grade; do not add grain, tint, glow, or vignetting automatically.

Build from **macro contact/texture**, **hands-in-frame use**, and **wider context**, pushed into the bolder angles above. Macro framing should show the contact point and material; a wider view establishes the object and result. Keep hands clean, anatomically plausible, with short or neatly manicured nails, restrained jewelry, and confident, practiced handling; viewers respond to skilled manipulation and are put off by fumbling or stiff, performed motion. Frame faces, mouths, and presenter performance out entirely. Do not ask the camera to pass through an object or reveal unsupported sides.

### Write the timestamped script
